A square wave is about as un-circular a shape as you can draw. It sits flat,
jumps, sits flat again. Circles do nothing but curve. So it's a genuinely
strange fact that you can build the first thing entirely out of the second.

Here is the whole trick in one picture. Start with one circle turning at a
steady rate, and trace the height of its rim over time — that's a sine wave.
Now attach a second, smaller circle to the rim of the first, turning three
times as fast. Then a third turning five times as fast, and so on. Drag the
slider to add more:

<FourierSquareWave class="wide" />

With one circle you get a plain sine wave. By three or four, the flat stretches
have started to appear. By fifteen, it's unmistakably a square wave with the
corners slightly rounded off.

## The part that doesn't go away

Watch the corners as you add circles. The wobble narrows, and it moves closer
to the jump — but it never shrinks. There is always an overshoot of about nine
percent right at the discontinuity, no matter how many terms you add.

This is the Gibbs phenomenon, and it's the reason the picture above is worth
looking at rather than just reading about. The series really does converge to
the square wave at every individual point; the overshoot survives by retreating
into an ever-narrower sliver next to the jump, which is exactly the kind of
thing that is hard to believe from an inequality and obvious from an animation.

## Why odd multiples

The frequencies go 1, 3, 5, 7 — never 2, 4, 6. A square wave is antisymmetric
about its midpoint: shift it half a period and you get its negative. The even
harmonics don't have that property, so they can't contribute anything, and
their coefficients come out to exactly zero.

The amplitudes fall off as one over the frequency. That's slow, which is why it
takes so many circles to get a decent corner — sharp features are expensive in
this currency.
